Miku

A pupil that knows only what you taught it, so you can see what you actually conveyed.

The question

I am interested in the distance between feeling that you understand something and actually understanding it. That distance is usually invisible to the person standing in it: the feeling arrives first and arrives whole, and nothing contradicts it until a test does.

Generative AI widens it. A model will produce a fluent answer to a question you could not have answered yourself, and reading a fluent answer feels a great deal like knowing. The question I started from was whether a tool could make that gap visible to a learner while they were still in it, rather than afterwards.

My approach

Teaching is the oldest test of understanding, because a gap you can talk past in your own head becomes a sentence you cannot finish out loud. So the learner teaches rather than answers.

The pupil is deliberately blank. Miku has no knowledge of its own and answers only from what it has been taught, which means every one of its mistakes traces back to something the learner left out, said unclearly, or believed wrongly. A pupil that could fill its own gaps would hide theirs.

The second decision is the prediction. Before each question, the learner says whether they think Miku will get it right. Teaching alone shows what was conveyed; the prediction turns that into a measure of self-knowledge, because the interesting quantity is not the score but the distance between the expected score and the real one.

What I made

A daily curriculum the learner teaches from, a teaching interface, an item-level prediction step, and a map returned at the end showing where prediction and result agreed and where they came apart.

What changed

Miku is the third attempt and the first one I have kept.

The first was a reflection chatbot. In an early parent interview a father told me, with some relief, that he had been out of school too long to answer his child's questions and now the chatbot could. He meant it kindly. I had built something a parent could use to step back, and I had not noticed.

The second was a story-relay game, adapted from a Berkeley project, where children reasoned through fictional scenarios. I set it aside before any child used it. Extending a story rewards building on an idea, and the habit I wanted to build was questioning one.

Working in Dr Sakshi Ghai's lab changed the first design again: a chatbot that invites a child to confide in an AI is a safeguarding decision, not only an interaction one.

Evidence, and what it does not show

Adult friends have used the prototype. I am interviewing parents before any child uses it.

That is the whole of it. There is no child data, no comparison condition and no measure of whether calibration improved or held outside the session. Nothing here shows that Miku teaches anyone anything. What it shows is that the mechanism runs and that adults can complete a session without me sitting next to them.

Open questions

The one I cannot yet answer is whether the prediction step helps or hurts. Asking someone to commit to a judgement before they see the result may sharpen their attention, or may anchor them to a first guess they then defend. Dr Yana Litovsky's work on guessing and fluency is what put the question in front of me, and it is a question about my design rather than a finding of hers.

This case study is still being written. What is above is the whole of what is claimed so far; the sections still to come are the detail, not a different story.
