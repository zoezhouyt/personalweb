03Prefrontal cortex · judgement

The question I keep returning to

Calibration is the fit between how confident a learner is and how well they actually perform. It sits underneath most of the decisions a learner makes on their own: when to stop revising, when to ask for help, when to accept an answer that arrived too easily.

Generative AI has made the question urgent. A fluent answer feels like understanding, and that feeling arrives whether or not the understanding does. My dissertation asks when AI supports a learner's judgement and when it quietly replaces the work that would have built it.

  • Dissertation, supervised by Dr Luc SchneiderNov 2026 – present

    Generative AI and metacognitive judgement: the conditions under which an AI assistant supports a learner's own judgement rather than standing in for it.

  • Research apprenticeship to Dr Yana LitovskyOct 2026 – present

    Curiosity, guess generation and susceptibility to misleading claims, at LSE. The work made me question one of my own design decisions: whether asking a learner to predict their performance anchors them to a judgement they then defend.

Miku

A teachable agent that turns a learner's own prediction into a calibration measure.

Working prototype · no child data yet

The problem
A learner who can produce a fluent answer will usually believe they understand it. Struggling to produce something used to be the signal that you did not, and a generative model removes that signal while leaving the work looking finished. Confidence goes up, calibration goes down, and nobody in the room can see it happening.
My role
Founder. I set the product direction, wrote the ethical design framework and the curriculum architecture, and moved the design away from a child-facing chatbot towards something a school could put in front of a class.
What it rests on
The teachable-agent tradition: a learner who prepares to teach, and then teaches, understands more than one who prepares only for themselves. Betty's Brain is the best-known system in this line, and the protegé effect is the result behind it. The calibration half comes from the metacognitive judgement literature, where the measure of interest is the distance between predicted and actual performance.
What I built
The learner teaches Miku, who is deliberately blank and answers only from what it has been taught. The learner then predicts, question by question, what Miku will get right, including questions Miku was never taught to handle. The distance between prediction and result is the calibration score, and it is shown back as a map of what the learner actually managed to convey.
Evidence, and its limits
Adult friends have used the prototype and it behaves as designed. I am interviewing parents before any child uses it.
So there is no child data, no evidence that calibration improves, and no evidence of transfer. What exists is a working system and adult feedback.
What I would test next
Whether the prediction step improves calibration or anchors it. The work with Dr Litovsky on guess generation raised the possibility that asking for an early judgement makes a learner defend it. The cleanest first study compares learners who teach Miku and predict against learners who teach Miku and do not, with calibration measured before and after. That isolates the prediction mechanic from the teaching itself.

LinkSea

The student-facing interface of a school workflow platform, rebuilt on what the students said.

Shipped · Hong Kong middle school

The problem
A middle school was consolidating its internal processes onto one platform used by students, teachers and administrators. Each group needed a different view of the same data. I owned the student view.
My role
Designed and built the student interface in JavaScript, one of three, and kept it aligned with the backend owner as the data model moved underneath it.
What it rests on
Nothing exotic. Watch the people who will use a thing attempt the task, in their own setting, before deciding what the screen should say.
What I built
The student-side flows, then field testing and qualitative interviews with the students themselves, then a rebuild of the parts they could not complete.
Evidence, and its limits
The field testing changed the design, and I can say which parts changed and why.
I did not collect task completion rates before and after, so I cannot put a number on the improvement. At the time it did not occur to me, and that is the specific thing I would do differently.
What I would test next
Time to complete and failure rate on the three tasks students do most often, measured on the old interface and the new one with the same cohort.

Noetic

One-day builds designed so that ability shows up before a track record does.

Running · co-founded 2026

The problem
People with real ability and no track record have very few places to demonstrate it, and the usual filters select for the track record. A one-day build is a learning environment with an unusually short feedback loop, which makes it a good place to test whether a different filter works.
My role
Co-founder. I wrote the commercial thesis, the competitive landscape and the sponsor proposition, and designed how the days themselves run.
What it rests on
Two decisions I can defend. Tracks are split by experience, so a first-timer is never scored against someone who has already shipped, which lowers the cost of trying something in public. Judging rewards whether a team can explain why they chose a particular model, ahead of how finished the result looks, because the reasoning is the part worth training.
What I built
The format: students and engineers working with a frontier company's newest tools, with that company's own engineers in the room for the day.
The first version of it served only the learners, and a large company went quiet for weeks. Startups said yes, because a first hands-on day with their tools is the adoption their go-to-market already needs.
Then the operating layer, after the first run depended entirely on me. Retrospectives became proposal templates, selection criteria and written procedures, and volunteers run them now.
Evidence, and its limits
The Nebius day drew 103 applicants. We selected 63, and 63 came.
Those are attendance numbers. They show the format is attractive and that people who commit turn up. They say nothing about whether anyone learned something that lasted past the evening, which is the question I care about.
What I would test next
Whether participants use the tools again. A short follow-up at four weeks asking what they have built since, set against a comparison group who attended a day without the engineers present, would separate the access from the event.
