About

Beijing · Abu Dhabi · London

My English changed shape three times. In Beijing I spoke American, copied from a teacher. At boarding school in England it turned British inside a year.1 In Abu Dhabi, with stretches in Riyadh, I could not copy the accent at all, so I copied something else: the manner of the Emirati women who were friends of my parents, who were courteous to everyone and certain when they spoke.

They were the ones who asked what I wanted to study, what I should go and see, and what I would need to show before anyone took an idea of mine seriously.

Moving that often teaches you that the same explanation does not land the same way twice. It is probably why I ended up studying how people judge what they know,2 and why I am wary of a learning design that works in one classroom and is assumed to travel.

The question I keep returning to is calibration: the fit between how confident a learner is and how well they actually perform. Generative AI has made it urgent. A fluent answer feels like understanding, and that feeling arrives whether or not the understanding does.3

So I build things that try to put the gap back where a learner can see it,4 and I spend the rest of my time on the environments people learn in.5 I have stopped two prototypes on the way here, which is the more useful half of the story.

There was also a year in private equity,6 which taught me how a market puts a number on a product. Seeing one valued from the other side of the table changed how I think about building.

Miku

Current

Founder

A teachable agent. The learner teaches Miku, who is deliberately blank and answers only from what it has been taught, then predicts question by question what Miku will get right. The distance between prediction and result is the calibration measure, shown back as a map of what the learner actually managed to convey.

Two prototypes came before it. The first ran into a problem I had not considered. The second into one I had, and had got backwards.

  • 01
    A reflective chatbot, stopped

    Started at Stanford GSE's AI Tinkery. A father told me he was relieved: he could not answer his child's questions, and now something could. His relief unsettled me. I had built a tool a parent could use to step back.

  • 02
    A story-relay game, stopped

    Adapted from a Berkeley project. I set it aside before any child used it. Extending a story rewards building on an idea, and the habit I wanted to train was questioning one.

  • 03
    Where Miku stands

    Adult friends have used it. I am interviewing parents before any child does. No child data yet, so no claim that it works.
